The Full Story

An expansion of a park that already exists.

Deltona Corp Comprehensive Plan Amendment — Holder Industrial Park Expansion
Withdrawn by Deltona · June 23, 2026
Holder Industrial Park, Lecanto 557 → 813 acres

Deltona Corporation — represented by attorney Sid Ansbacher — filed a comprehensive plan amendment, since withdrawn, that sought to expand the Holder Industrial Park in northern Citrus County, near County Road 491 and Tram Road in Lecanto. The park already includes approximately 557 acres of land zoned for industrial use. The amendment would expand that footprint to roughly 813 acres and add information processing, data center utilities, and data storage as permitted uses under the site's land-use rules. The site has had state backing for infrastructure buildout: in 2024, Gov. DeSantis's administration provided a $2.8 million grant for wastewater connections.

County Commissioner Jeff Kinnard has described the project as a potential economic boost, estimating 100–150 operational jobs if a data center is built. The developer's projections are larger: approximately 2,500 construction jobs, 825 permanent positions, and $105 million in property tax revenue over time. These numbers are developer estimates — as at comparable projects in Polk County and elsewhere, independent analysts have disputed the sustainability of the job counts given how few operational staff hyperscale data centers actually employ. Kinnard has publicly framed the amendment as "a simple rezoning to expand our largest industrial park" and said the application specifically allows for a data center because that's what current interest indicates.

Citrus moved in an order most Florida counties don't. On February 21, 2026, commissioners voted 5–0 to instruct staff to draft land-development-code guidelines specifically for data centers — water use, noise, energy demand — before acting on Deltona's application. The Planning and Development Commission heard the amendment on March 5, 2026, and the final vote was pushed off. Then on May 26 commissioners adopted a 12-month moratorium 5–0. Deltona's application, already in the queue, was exempt from it — so the county set a July 14 hearing. Deltona asked to move that hearing past the November election; commissioners refused unanimously on June 22; and on June 23 Deltona withdrew both applications. Withdrawal, not denial — which means Deltona surrendered its exemption. Any return is a new filing, and new filings are frozen.

Residents have organized at county meetings. Cora and Gary Engstrom, whose 20-acre parcel sits surrounded by Deltona parcels proposed for rezoning, have been among the most visible opponents. At a public meeting, Cora Engstrom said the project would disrupt the solitude the family has lived with for years. Gary Engstrom described the area as "the nature coast" and "God's country," framing industrial expansion as incompatible with the character that drew residents to the area. Hundreds have signed petitions against the rezoning. Deltona has declined to comment in most press interviews.

Timeline

How we got here.

2024
State infrastructure grant. Gov. DeSantis's administration provides a $2.8 million grant to Citrus County to complete wastewater connections at the Holder Industrial Park, laying the groundwork for expanded industrial use at the site.
January 2026
Deltona Corp files amendment request. Attorney Sid Ansbacher, representing Deltona Corporation, files a request with Citrus County for a comprehensive plan amendment covering roughly 813 acres at the Holder Industrial Park site, expanding existing industrial acreage and adding data center uses.
February 21, 2026
Commissioners vote 5-0 to draft data center guidelines. Citrus County commissioners instruct staff to draft land-development-code guidelines specific to data centers — addressing water use, noise, and energy demand — before acting on the Deltona amendment.
March 5, 2026
Planning and Development Commission hearing. The PDC considers the Deltona amendment and recommends next steps. County commissioners were expected to consider the proposal in April.
March 9, 2026
Final vote postponed. Citrus commissioners push the final rezoning vote off by several weeks. The developer tells press it plans to wait for state legislation and base any new proposal on what passes. The application would ultimately be scheduled for July 14 — and withdrawn before it was ever heard.
May 26, 2026
Commission adopts a 12-month moratorium, 5–0. A packed chamber and over 100 public speakers — not one spoke in favor. The ordinance freezes new data center rezonings, development orders, and building permits in unincorporated Citrus while staff drafts regulations. Many residents demanded an outright ban; commissioners said they legally couldn't. “We cannot do a permanent moratorium,” Commissioner Jeff Kinnard said. “That would get the county sued and we would likely lose.” Deltona's application, filed earlier, was not covered.
June 17, 2026
Deltona asks to delay the vote until after the election. The night before the Planning & Development Commission hearing, Deltona's attorney sends the county a letter requesting that the July 14 County Commission hearing be continued until November — after the county commission election.
June 18, 2026
Planning & Development Commission recommends denial, unanimously. A hearing running roughly nine hours draws more than 200 residents, the vast majority opposed. Concerns center on the Floridan Aquifer, drinking water wells, noise, traffic, emergency services, and wildlife. Intervenor Joe Hicks, a Beverly Hills resident and data center infrastructure expert, tells the commission the revised 777-acre “Data Center Use Area” could support 1,800–2,700 megawatts, and that Deltona's May 12 revision stripped out water protections discussed earlier.
June 22, 2026
Commissioners deny the delay, unanimously. Chair Diana Finegan leads the effort to reject Deltona's request to push the hearing past November.
June 23, 2026
Deltona withdraws both applications. “On behalf of our client, Deltona Corporation, we respectfully submit this formal notice of withdrawal of applications CPA-2-2025-000012 and CPA 2025-000013 concerning the Holder Industrial Park.” The July 14 hearings are canceled. Because the applications are withdrawn rather than denied, Deltona loses its place in the process — any new filing starts over and falls under the moratorium. “I think this is good news because now they are under the moratorium for data centers,” Commissioner Holly Davis said. Finegan cautioned Deltona could return with a different proposal, but said the county expects stronger regulations in place first.
What It Means

What to watch in Citrus County.

The "expansion" framing

Deltona and Commissioner Kinnard have publicly framed the amendment as a relatively modest expansion of an existing industrial park — not a new use at a new site. That framing has a procedural advantage: expanding an already-industrial parcel is typically less controversial than rezoning raw agricultural or conservation land. The substantive change is adding data center as a permitted use, which is a separate issue from whether the park grows. That distinction still matters if Deltona returns: the acreage question and the allowed-use question are separate, and only one of them was ever the real fight — acreage expansion and allowed-use expansion — as related but distinct decisions.

Why drafting guidelines first worked

Citrus reversed the default order and it decided the outcome. Most Florida counties act on the first big data center application, then try to write rules afterward — Fort Meade approved first and is now being sued. Citrus voted on February 21 to write standards before acting, then backed it with a moratorium on May 26. That sequence is what made Deltona's withdrawal terminal rather than tactical: because the moratorium was already law when Deltona pulled out, the exemption it was relying on evaporated with the application. “I think this is good news because now they are under the moratorium for data centers,” Commissioner Holly Davis said. The guidelines-drafting process is still the thing to watch — whatever standards exist when the freeze lapses are what actually govern.

The Engstrom parcel

The Engstrom family's 20-acre parcel, surrounded by Deltona parcels proposed for rezoning, is the textbook case of an incompatible adjacency. Under Florida land-use law, neighboring property owners are typically afforded standing to oppose rezonings that materially change land character around their parcels. A 100-foot industrial buffer — the figure the Engstroms have publicly disputed — is substantially smaller than the 1,500-2,000-foot distance at which noise and traffic impacts begin to drop in practice for large data centers.

The “run out the clock” move

Deltona first said it would wait for Florida's state data center legislation — a common developer play in 2026, since it avoids a public vote during peak opposition and lets the next proposal be shaped around whatever confidentiality and permitting shields the state law provides. SB 484 took effect July 1, 2026. When that wasn't enough, Deltona tried the more direct version: the night before the June 18 hearing, its attorney asked the county to continue the July 14 vote until after the November county commission election — an election in which the group No Data Centers Citrus is actively backing candidates. Commissioners denied it unanimously. Deltona withdrew the next day. The tactic is worth naming because it will be tried again elsewhere: when a developer asks to delay a vote past an election, the delay is the strategy.

Citrus County sits on Florida's Nature Coast, with significant protected natural resources including Crystal River and the Chassahowitzka Wildlife Management Area. The guidelines the county writes this year will shape how much protection those resources retain against the next wave of applications — not just Deltona's.
